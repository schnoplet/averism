

A claim is stronger when there is some possible observation that could show it to be wrong.

This matters because a claim that can explain absolutely anything does not tell us very much.

Suppose someone says that an invisible being controls every event in the universe, but also says that no possible observation could ever show this to be false. There is no clear way to test the claim. Whatever happens can simply be explained as part of the claim.

Averism treats this as a problem.

When an Averist makes a claim that can be tested, they should be able to describe what evidence would count against it.

For example, if an Averist claims that a certain event has a natural explanation, evidence of a different cause should reduce confidence in that claim.

Some questions cannot currently be tested. Questions about ultimate reality, subjective experience, or what may happen after death can reach the limits of what humans are able to observe. Those questions are still worth discussing, but an Averist should be clear about which parts are evidence-based and which parts are philosophical conclusions or speculation.

A claim should not be made artificially difficult to disprove.

Moving the standard every time contrary evidence appears is not a genuine test. Neither is changing the meaning of a claim after it has failed.

Averism therefore encourages its followers to state, when possible:

**What would change my mind?**

This question should be asked before becoming deeply attached to a belief.

If there is genuinely no possible evidence that would ever change the claim, then the claim may belong to a different category from an ordinary factual belief. It may be a value, definition, personal commitment, or philosophical assumption.

Knowing the difference helps prevent confusion.

Averism does not require every belief to be experimentally testable.

It does require that testable claims remain open to being tested.

**A claim that cannot possibly be wrong cannot gain support from evidence either.**
