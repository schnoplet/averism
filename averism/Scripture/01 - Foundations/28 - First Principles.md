
When investigating a difficult question, it can help to separate what is actually known from what has simply been assumed.

A first principle is a basic starting point that is used to reason about other things.

Some principles come from definitions. Others come from observations or from reasoning that has already been examined.

Averists should be careful about treating assumptions as established facts.

For example, an argument might begin by assuming that human perception is generally reliable. That assumption may be reasonable, because much of what we know depends on perception. But it is still worth asking why we trust it and where its limits are.

The same applies to assumptions about the world.

We may assume that the past existed, that other people have minds, or that the world continues to exist when nobody is observing it. These assumptions are deeply connected to ordinary life and to almost all investigation.

Averism should identify important assumptions rather than hiding them.

This becomes especially important when dealing with questions about reality itself.

If an argument depends on a principle that has not been established, the uncertainty of that principle should be acknowledged.

This does not mean that every argument must begin from absolute certainty. That would make almost all reasoning impossible.

It means that the foundations of an argument should be visible.

When two people disagree about a conclusion, they may actually agree on the evidence and disagree about a starting assumption.

Finding that difference can make the disagreement much clearer.

First principles should also be open to examination. A principle is not beyond criticism simply because many other arguments depend on it.

Some principles may survive repeated examination and become very difficult to reasonably reject.

Others may remain uncertain.

Averism should distinguish between the two.

**Know what your argument is built on before deciding how strong the argument is.**
