

People do not examine ideas from a completely neutral position.

Our experiences, emotions, relationships, fears, and expectations can affect how we interpret information. We may notice evidence that supports what we already believe and overlook evidence that does not.

This can happen without a person realising it.

Averism therefore asks its followers to consider their own motives when evaluating an important claim.

A person should ask whether they want a particular conclusion to be true, whether they would judge the same evidence differently if it supported another position, and whether they are giving enough attention to information that challenges them.

This does not mean that every belief caused by emotion is false.

Fear can lead someone to notice a real danger. Compassion can lead someone to recognise genuine suffering. Personal experience can provide useful information.

The problem begins when a person's feelings are treated as evidence for a conclusion that they have not actually established.

Other people can have biases too, but assuming that another person's disagreement is caused by bias is not a substitute for answering their argument.

It is possible to be biased and still be correct.

It is also possible to have good intentions and still reach a false conclusion.

Averists should therefore examine their own reasoning before trying to diagnose the motives of someone else.

When possible, a person should actively look for information that could change their mind.

They should also be willing to apply the same standards to evidence they like and evidence they dislike.

This is difficult because beliefs can become connected to identity.

A person may begin to feel that admitting an error means admitting something bad about themselves. It does not.

A wrong conclusion can be corrected.

Changing your mind does not erase the effort that led you there. It means the conclusion has been updated.

Averism asks its followers to care about whether their beliefs are accurate more than whether those beliefs make them feel secure.

**Before asking whether someone else is biased, examine whether you might be doing the same thing.**
