

When a person makes a claim, they should have some reason for others to believe it.

The person making the claim carries the burden of providing evidence for it. Another person should not be expected to disprove every claim that someone can imagine.

For example, anyone could claim that an invisible object is orbiting the Earth. The fact that nobody can prove that it is not there would not be a good reason to believe that it is.

This does not mean that every claim requires the same amount of evidence.

A small claim may need very little evidence. A claim that would change our understanding of the universe requires much stronger evidence.

The consequences of a belief also matter. If acting on a claim could seriously harm someone, an Averist should be more careful about accepting it without strong evidence.

The burden of proof can also change when evidence is provided.

If someone claims that a particular event happened and produces reliable evidence that it did, others have a reason to take the claim seriously. They may still question the evidence or offer another explanation, but they should respond to the evidence rather than pretending it was never given.

A person should also distinguish between saying:

**“I have no reason to believe this.”**

and:

**“I have reason to believe this is false.”**

Those are different claims.

Not having enough evidence to accept an idea does not automatically prove that the idea is wrong.

An Averist should therefore avoid both extremes: accepting claims without sufficient evidence, and declaring claims false simply because they have not been proven true.

The appropriate response depends on the evidence available.

**The person making a claim is responsible for giving us a reason to believe it.**
