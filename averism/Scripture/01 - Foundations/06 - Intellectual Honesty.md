

An Averist should try to represent their beliefs accurately.

This means saying what the evidence actually supports, even when the full answer is less satisfying than the answer they wanted.

It also means being honest about the difference between an observation and an interpretation.

For example, “I saw a light in the sky” describes an observation. “The light was a supernatural sign” is an interpretation of that observation. The second claim needs its own reasons.

An Averist should also avoid making an argument sound stronger than it is. Leaving out important evidence, using misleading examples, or presenting uncertainty as certainty can make a weak position look convincing without actually improving it.

This applies when arguing against an idea too.

A person should try to understand a position before criticizing it. If the strongest version of an argument is easier to defeat than a distorted version, the stronger version should be addressed.

When someone finds a mistake in their own argument, they should correct it.

When someone else points out a genuine error, the goal should be to determine whether the criticism is correct rather than simply finding a way to defend the original position.

Intellectual honesty also means admitting when someone else has a better argument.

There is no shame in saying:

“I was wrong.”

“I don't have enough evidence.”

“That argument is stronger than mine.”

“I don't know.”

These statements can be difficult to say, especially when other people are watching.

They are still preferable to pretending to know something that you do not know.

An Averist should care more about reaching the correct conclusion than about winning an argument.

**A belief is not strengthened by hiding the reasons it might be wrong.**
