
Words can cause arguments even when people are actually disagreeing about something much smaller.

Averists should therefore define important terms before using them in an argument.

A word can have several meanings in ordinary language. If two people use different meanings without realising it, they can appear to disagree when they do not.

This is especially important for words such as _knowledge_, _truth_, _belief_, _consciousness_, _good_, _evil_, _God_, _cause_, and _evidence_.

Definitions should be clear enough that another person can understand what is meant without having to guess.

A definition should also be used consistently.

If the meaning of a word changes halfway through an argument, the conclusion may appear stronger than it really is.

This does not mean that every word needs one permanent definition. Language changes, and technical subjects often give ordinary words more specific meanings.

When Averism gives a word a specific meaning, that meaning should be stated clearly.

Averists should also avoid arguing over definitions when the real disagreement is about something else.

For example, two people might use the word _knowledge_ differently while agreeing completely about what information a person should trust. In that case, changing the definition will not resolve the underlying disagreement.

Definitions are tools for making ideas clearer.

They are not evidence for the ideas themselves.

Calling something “good” does not prove that it is good. Calling something “true” does not make it true.

An Averist should therefore separate the meaning of a term from the claim being made with that term.

Clear language makes mistakes easier to find.

It also makes agreement easier to recognise.

**Before arguing about an important claim, make sure everyone means the same thing by the words being used.**
