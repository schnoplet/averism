

When several explanations fit the same evidence, an Averist should consider how many assumptions each explanation requires.

An explanation that requires fewer unsupported assumptions is generally preferable to one that requires many.

This does not mean that the simplest explanation is always correct. Reality can be complicated. A complicated explanation can be right when the evidence requires it.

The point is to avoid adding things that are not needed.

Suppose a person sees that a glass has broken on the floor. There are many possible explanations. Someone dropped it. It fell from a table. An animal knocked it over. A person deliberately smashed it.

The evidence available might support one explanation more strongly than the others.

An explanation should therefore be judged by how well it accounts for what was actually observed, not by how interesting or complicated it is.

Averists should also be careful about explanations that can be adjusted indefinitely.

If every possible observation can be explained by adding another assumption, the explanation may become impossible to test meaningfully.

For example, if a prediction fails and the explanation is changed by adding a new condition every time, the explanation may appear to survive while becoming less useful.

Simplicity is therefore a useful principle when comparing explanations, but it is not an absolute rule.

An Averist should prefer an explanation that accounts for the evidence well without unnecessary assumptions, while remaining willing to accept a more complicated explanation when the evidence supports it.

The question is not:

**“Which explanation sounds simplest?”**

The question is:

**“Which explanation explains the evidence best, while assuming only what we have reason to assume?”**
