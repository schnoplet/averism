

Evidence is information that gives us a reason to believe that something is true or false.

Not all evidence is equally reliable.

A well-controlled experiment is generally stronger evidence for a physical claim than a person's memory of an event from many years ago. A record made at the time of an event may be more useful than a story that was passed down through several generations. A repeated observation is usually more useful than a single unusual experience.

Averism does not require every claim to have the same kind of evidence. Different questions require different kinds of evidence.

When judging evidence, an Averist should ask where it came from, how reliable it is, whether it can be independently checked, and whether there are other explanations that fit the same evidence.

Evidence can also be incomplete.

The absence of evidence is not always evidence of absence. If we would expect to find something if a claim were true, and we look carefully and do not find it, that can count against the claim. If we had little reason to expect to find it in the first place, the absence tells us much less.

Personal experience deserves consideration, but it should be treated carefully. People can misremember events, misunderstand what happened, notice patterns that are not really there, and become convinced of things that later turn out to have ordinary explanations.

The same applies to testimony. A person can be honest and still be mistaken.

Evidence should therefore be considered alongside other evidence rather than treated as automatically decisive.

An Averist should also try to look for evidence that disagrees with their current belief. Only looking for information that supports a conclusion makes it much easier to become convinced of something that is false.

The strength of a belief should reflect the strength of the evidence behind it.

When the evidence is strong, confidence can be high.

When the evidence is weak or conflicting, confidence should be lower.

When there is not enough evidence to reach a reasonable conclusion, the appropriate answer is **uncertain**.

This is not an excuse to avoid difficult questions. It is a way of being honest about what we actually know.
