

Some questions are about what is true.

Others are about what ought to be done.

These questions are connected, but they are not the same.

A fact is a claim about how something is. A value is a judgement about what is good, bad, important, desirable, or worth protecting.

For example, “People can feel pain” is a factual claim.

“People should avoid causing unnecessary pain” is a value.

The second claim can depend on the first, but the first does not automatically prove the second. There has to be some principle connecting them.

This distinction is important because arguments can become confusing when facts and values are mixed together.

A person might prove that something causes harm without proving that harm is morally wrong. They may have good reasons for considering harm wrong, but those reasons need to be stated.

Averists should therefore make their moral assumptions clear.

This also applies in the other direction. A person may strongly believe that something is morally good while being mistaken about what will actually produce that result.

Good intentions do not make false factual claims true.

When making an argument, an Averist should try to identify:

**What do we know?**

**What are we assuming?**

**What value or goal are we using?**

**What conclusion follows from those things?**

This does not make moral questions purely mathematical. Human values can be complicated, and reasonable people can disagree about them.

It does mean that disagreements can be made clearer.

Two people may agree about the facts and disagree about what should be valued. Or they may agree about the value but disagree about the facts.

Those are different disagreements and should be treated differently.

Averism should not pretend that every moral question can be solved by facts alone.

It should also not treat moral beliefs as beyond criticism.

**Know whether you are describing what is, or arguing about what ought to be.**
