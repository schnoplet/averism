

Averism requires its beliefs to be consistent with one another.

If two claims contradict each other, at least one of them must be wrong unless there is some important difference between the claims that has been overlooked.

This is especially important when a belief is applied to other people.

A principle should not change simply because the person affected by it is a friend, stranger, follower, critic, or leader. Personal loyalty can influence our judgement without giving us a good reason to change the standard.

The same applies to evidence.

Evidence that counts against a belief should not be dismissed for a reason that would also dismiss evidence supporting the belief. A standard should work in both directions.

For example, if a person considers a personal experience strong evidence when it supports their religion, but weak evidence when someone else has the same kind of experience supporting another religion, they are using different standards without a good reason.

Averists should look for these inconsistencies in themselves as well as in others.

This does not mean every situation will have an identical answer. Context can matter. Two situations that look similar may have important differences. Those differences should be explained rather than assumed.

Consistency also applies to the foundations of Averism itself.

The principles used to evaluate other beliefs must also be used to evaluate Averism. If a method produces a result that is inconvenient for Averism, that result should still be taken seriously.

Averism cannot claim that evidence and reasoning matter while making exceptions whenever they threaten an Averist conclusion.

A belief system that changes its standards depending on the conclusion it wants is difficult to trust.

**Use the same standard when the answer helps you and when it does not.**
