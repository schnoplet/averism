

People often trust ideas because they come from someone they respect.

A parent may teach a child what is true. A teacher may explain something they understand well. A scientist may have spent years studying a subject. A religious leader may have knowledge that other people do not.

This kind of trust can be useful.

It is not enough by itself to establish that a claim is true.

A person can be an expert and still make a mistake. A respected teacher can misunderstand something. A tradition can preserve useful knowledge, but it can also preserve an error.

Averism therefore does not give a claim extra truth simply because it came from an authority.

Expertise should still matter when judging evidence. Someone with years of relevant training will usually be better placed to explain a technical subject than someone with no experience in it. The important point is that their expertise supports their reliability; it does not make them incapable of being wrong.

Tradition should be treated in a similar way.

An old idea may deserve attention because it has survived criticism, because many people have examined it, or because it contains useful historical evidence. Its age alone does not make it true.

The same standard applies to the founders and scripture of Averism.

A founder can be wrong.

A passage in the Canon can be wrong.

A teaching can become outdated.

None of these should be protected from examination simply because they are part of the religion.

An Averist may respect a teacher without agreeing with them. They may value a tradition without treating it as unquestionable.

A good question is therefore not:

**“Who said this?”**

It is:

**“Why should I believe it?”**

Knowing who said something can help answer that question.

It should not end the investigation.
