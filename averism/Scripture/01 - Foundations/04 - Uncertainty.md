

There are things we know, things we have good reason to believe, and things we do not know.

Averism does not treat these as the same.

A person might know that they exist because they are directly experiencing their own thoughts. They might have strong evidence that the Earth is round. They might have a reasonable belief about what happened at a historical event. They might have almost no useful information about what happens after death.

All four are different situations.

Averists should try to recognize which situation they are in.

This matters because people often become more certain than their evidence allows. A guess can slowly become an opinion, an opinion can become a firm belief, and eventually the person may forget that the whole thing started as a guess.

Uncertainty should be stated when it matters.

This does not mean refusing to make decisions until perfect knowledge is available. We often have to act using incomplete information. When that happens, the decision should be based on the evidence available at the time, while recognising that it could be wrong.

A belief can also have different levels of confidence.

For example:

**Certain** means there is no reasonable basis for doubting the claim.

**Very likely** means the evidence strongly supports it, although being wrong remains possible.

**Likely** means it currently has more support than the alternatives.

**Open** means the available evidence does not clearly favour one conclusion.

**Unlikely** means the evidence currently weighs against it.

**Very unlikely** means there is strong evidence against it.

These labels are not permanent. A claim can move from one level to another when new evidence appears.

An Averist should never confuse confidence with truth.

Being 99% confident does not make a claim true. It means that, given what is currently known, the person thinks the claim is very likely to be true.

The difference may seem small in everyday life, but it becomes important when dealing with questions that have serious consequences.

Averism therefore allows the phrase **“I don't know.”**

It is sometimes the most accurate answer available.

When that is the case, admitting uncertainty is part of honest belief rather than a reason to fill the gap with an unsupported answer.
