

Averists will disagree.

This is expected. People have different experiences, information, assumptions, and ways of interpreting evidence. Even when two people have access to the same information, they may reach different conclusions.

Disagreement does not mean that one person is dishonest.

An Averist should try to understand another person's position before responding to it. Misrepresenting someone's argument makes it easier to attack, but it does not help determine whether the argument is correct.

A good disagreement should focus on the claim and the reasons supporting it.

Insults, ridicule, threats, and pressure do not make an argument stronger.

An Averist should also be willing to recognise when a disagreement cannot currently be resolved. Two people may have different values, or there may simply not be enough evidence to settle the question.

In those situations, forcing agreement is unnecessary.

Good faith also applies when discussing beliefs outside Averism.

A person should not be treated as unintelligent or immoral simply because they belong to another religion, follow no religion, or reject Averism entirely. Their arguments should be judged on their merits.

An Averist is allowed to think that another belief is false.

They are not entitled to pretend that they have proved it false when they have not.

The same standard applies when Averism is criticised.

Criticism should be examined for whether it identifies a real problem. If it does, the problem should be taken seriously. If it does not, the response should address the actual argument rather than attacking the person making it.

The aim of disagreement is to improve our understanding.

Sometimes that means convincing someone else.

Sometimes it means discovering that they were right.

Sometimes it means discovering that neither side has enough information yet.

**A disagreement is useful when both people are trying to find out what is true.**
