


A useful way to evaluate a belief is to ask what it predicts.

A prediction is a claim about what should happen if an idea is correct. It can be about something that will happen in the future, something that can be observed now, or something that should be found when we investigate the past.

Predictions give us a way to compare ideas.

Suppose two explanations can account for everything that has already happened, but one of them predicts something that the other does not. If that prediction is later tested and turns out to be correct, the first explanation gains support.

If the prediction fails, confidence in the explanation should decrease.

A prediction should be stated clearly enough that its result can actually be judged. Changing the prediction after seeing the result weakens the test.

Averists should also be careful about vague predictions.

Saying that “something unusual will happen” is difficult to test because almost anything could satisfy it. A useful prediction should make clear what is expected, under what conditions, and what result would count as a failure.

Testing is not limited to science. Historical claims, philosophical arguments, and religious claims can sometimes make predictions or produce consequences that can be compared with evidence.

Not every belief will produce a useful prediction. Some claims may describe things that cannot currently be observed or tested. In those cases, an Averist should not pretend that the claim has been tested when it has not.

A failed prediction does not always prove that an entire belief system is false. The prediction may have depended on another assumption that turned out to be wrong. The important thing is to identify which part failed rather than immediately protecting the original conclusion.

Averism therefore treats successful predictions as useful evidence and failed predictions as useful information.

A belief should not be judged only by how well it explains what has already happened.

It should also be asked what it would lead us to expect next.

**A good explanation should take a risk. It should give us something that could turn out to be wrong.**
