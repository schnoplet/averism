

Before deciding whether a belief is justified, it helps to distinguish knowledge from assumption.

Knowledge is information that has enough support to justify confidence in it. The exact standard can depend on the question.

Some things can be known directly through experience. For example, a person can know that they are currently seeing something because they are having that experience.

Other things are known through evidence and reasoning. A person does not need to personally observe every event in history to have good reason to believe that it happened.

This means knowledge does not require personal certainty.

A person can have strong enough evidence to reasonably call something known while still accepting that human error is possible.

Averism should therefore avoid treating knowledge as a perfect state in which a person can never be wrong.

There are also different kinds of claims.

A mathematical statement can be established through a formal proof.

A statement about the physical world normally depends on observation and evidence.

A statement about another person's thoughts may depend on what that person says and does.

A moral claim depends partly on the values and principles being used.

These should not all be judged by exactly the same method.

Averism should also distinguish between knowledge and familiarity.

Knowing a claim well does not make the claim true. Hearing the same idea many times can make it feel obvious even when it has never been properly supported.

Likewise, being unable to explain why you believe something does not automatically make it false. It means that the basis for the belief needs to be examined.

When a person says they know something, they should be able to explain what makes them confident.

The explanation does not need to be perfect.

It does need to be better than simply saying:

“Because I believe it.”

Knowledge is about the relationship between a belief and the reasons supporting it.

**The strength of a belief should depend on why it is believed.**
