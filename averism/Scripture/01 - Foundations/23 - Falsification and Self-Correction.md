

Averism should make it possible for its own beliefs to fail.

A belief that is protected from all possible evidence cannot be properly examined. It may still be meaningful to someone, but there is no reliable way to tell whether it is true.

When an Averist proposes an important claim, they should consider what evidence would make them reduce their confidence in it.

This should be done before the claim is tested whenever possible. Deciding what counts as failure only after seeing the result makes it much easier to protect a preferred conclusion.

If a prediction fails, the result should be recorded honestly.

There may be a good reason why it failed. The original claim may have depended on a mistaken assumption, the test may have been unreliable, or the evidence may have been misunderstood. These possibilities should be investigated.

What should not happen is quietly changing the claim so that the failed test no longer counts against it.

Averism should keep records of important corrections. When a teaching changes, the previous version should not simply disappear. The reason for the change should be recorded along with the evidence or argument that caused it.

This serves two purposes.

First, it helps Averists understand how their ideas developed.

Second, it makes it harder to rewrite the history of the religion to make its earlier beliefs look more accurate than they actually were.

Averism should be willing to say:

**“We believed this. We were wrong. Here is why we changed it.”**

That statement should be respected.

A religion that can correct itself has a better chance of remaining honest over time.

The same standard must apply to major claims about Averism.

If evidence seriously challenges a central teaching, Averists should investigate the challenge rather than assume the teaching must survive.

A belief is not made stronger by refusing to test it.

**A claim should have a way to fail, and a failed claim should be allowed to remain failed.**
