

An Averist should be willing to change a belief when the reasons for holding it no longer hold up.

This sounds simple, but it can be difficult in practice. People become attached to beliefs because those beliefs are connected to their family, their identity, their experiences, or the people they trust.

That attachment does not make the belief true.

When evidence changes, our understanding may need to change with it.

Changing a belief does not mean that the person was dishonest when they held it before. A person can reach a reasonable conclusion from the information they had and later discover that important information was missing.

For this reason, Averism does not treat being wrong as a moral failure.

Refusing to examine a belief because changing it would be uncomfortable is a different matter.

An Averist should be able to ask:

- What originally convinced me?
- Is that evidence still reliable?
- What evidence would make me change my mind?
- Have I considered the strongest argument against my position?
- Am I protecting the belief because I have good reasons, or because I want it to be true?

These questions should also be applied to Averism itself.

If a central teaching of Averism is shown to be false, Averists should be able to recognise that and change the teaching. A belief should not be protected from criticism because it is old, important, or considered sacred.

The same applies to this scripture.

The fact that an idea appears in the Canon does not make it immune from examination. The Canon is a record of Averist thought. It is not a replacement for reality.

Averism therefore has no special virtue in being consistent with its oldest ideas.

Its value depends on whether its ideas continue to be supported by good reasons.

**When better reasons appear, follow them.**
