

Two things happening together does not prove that one caused the other.

This is one of the easiest mistakes to make when looking at evidence.

If one event tends to happen whenever another event happens, they may be related. But there are several possible explanations.

One event may cause the other. The second may cause the first. Both may be caused by something else. Or the pattern may have happened by chance.

For example, suppose people who carry umbrellas are more likely to get wet. Carrying an umbrella did not cause the rain, and getting wet did not cause people to carry umbrellas. The rain affects both.

When an Averist claims that one thing causes another, they should look for evidence that actually supports a causal connection.

A useful question is:

**What would we expect to see if this really caused that?**

Controlled experiments can sometimes help because they allow researchers to change one factor while keeping other relevant factors as consistent as possible.

Experiments are not always possible, especially when investigating history, society, or events that cannot be repeated. In those cases, other forms of evidence can be used to build a case for causation.

Timing can also matter. A cause normally needs to happen before its effect. But happening first is not enough on its own.

Averists should be especially careful when a correlation confirms something they already want to believe.

A pattern can feel meaningful without actually showing a cause.

When the evidence only establishes that two things are associated, the conclusion should be stated that way.

Do not turn:

**“These things are related.”**

into:

**“This thing caused that thing.”**

without enough evidence to justify the change.

**A relationship between two events is evidence of a relationship. It is not automatically evidence of causation.**
