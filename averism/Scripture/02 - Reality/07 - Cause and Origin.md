

Things that happen often have causes.

A tree grows because of a combination of things such as its genetics, water, light, nutrients, and environment. A glass breaks because something caused enough force to break it.

This makes causation useful when trying to understand the world.

It also raises a much bigger question:

**Why does reality exist at all?**

There are several possibilities.

The universe may have had a beginning caused by something else.

The universe may have had a beginning without being caused in the ordinary sense.

The universe may not have had a beginning.

There may also be possibilities that we have not thought of.

Averism should not assume that the universe must have a cause simply because things within the universe usually have causes. The rule we observe inside reality may not apply in exactly the same way to reality as a whole.

Likewise, saying that the universe has a cause does not by itself tell us what that cause is.

It could be a physical process we do not yet understand. It could be something outside the universe as we normally understand it. It could be something else entirely.

There is also a problem with explanations that simply move the question somewhere else.

If someone says that the universe exists because of a cause, it is reasonable to ask why that cause exists. If they then say that the cause does not need an explanation, they should explain why that is different from saying the universe itself does not need one.

This does not prove that a first cause is impossible.

It means that arguments about origins need to deal with the same standards they apply to other explanations.

Averism therefore leaves the ultimate origin of reality open.

The question is important, but importance is not evidence.

We should follow the evidence as far as it goes and stop pretending to know what lies beyond it.

**The universe has an origin that we do not yet fully understand. What that origin is remains an open question.**
