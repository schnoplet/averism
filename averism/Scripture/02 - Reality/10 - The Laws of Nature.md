

The universe appears to follow patterns.

Objects fall according to predictable rules. Light behaves in consistent ways. Chemical reactions happen under particular conditions. The same physical laws appear to work across places and times that we can observe.

We call these regularities the laws of nature.

They allow us to predict what will happen.

But there is a difference between describing a law and explaining why that law exists.

For example, we can describe gravity mathematically and use those equations to make very accurate predictions. That does not automatically answer why gravity exists, why the laws have the form they do, or whether they could have been different.

These questions are deeper than the equations themselves.

Averism should avoid treating a successful description as a complete explanation.

At the same time, the fact that we do not know why a law exists does not give us evidence for a particular explanation.

It is tempting to fill gaps in knowledge with whatever explanation feels most satisfying. That can lead to conclusions that go beyond the evidence.

The laws of nature also place limits on what we should expect.

A claim that conflicts with extremely well-tested physical laws should require strong evidence. It should not be accepted merely because the event would be interesting or because someone believes it happened.

This does not make the laws of nature unquestionable.

Scientific laws are models based on observations. They can be improved when new evidence shows that they are incomplete.

Averism therefore treats the regular behaviour of nature as some of the strongest evidence we have about physical reality, while keeping questions about the ultimate basis of those laws open.

There may be deeper principles beneath the ones we currently understand.

There may not be.

At present, the evidence decides how far we can go.

**Knowing how nature behaves is not the same as knowing why nature has those rules.**
