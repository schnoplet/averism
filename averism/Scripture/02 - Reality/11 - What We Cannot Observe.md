

There are parts of reality that humans cannot currently observe directly.

The observable universe has limits. Light takes time to travel, so there are distant regions whose present state cannot yet be seen from Earth. There may also be things that cannot be detected with our current instruments.

This creates an important distinction between **what we have not observed** and **what does not exist**.

Not seeing something is not automatically evidence that it is absent.

The strength of that evidence depends on what we should expect to observe if the thing were actually there.

For example, if someone claims there is a large object sitting in an otherwise empty room, we would expect to see it. If we carefully inspect the room and find nothing, that is good evidence against the claim.

A claim about something far beyond our ability to detect is different. Failing to detect it may tell us very little.

This becomes especially important when discussing things outside ordinary physical observation.

Averism should not automatically accept such claims, but it should also avoid claiming that something is impossible simply because current instruments cannot detect it.

There is another limitation.

Some observations may be impossible even in principle. We may never be able to observe certain past events directly because they happened too long ago. We may never be able to observe another person's private experience in exactly the way they experience it themselves.

In those cases, indirect evidence becomes important.

We can study traces left by past events. We can compare independent reports. We can observe behaviour and examine the physical systems associated with it.

Indirect evidence can be strong.

It is still different from direct observation.

Averists should therefore state clearly when a conclusion depends on inference rather than direct measurement.

The boundary of observation is not necessarily the boundary of reality.

There may be more to reality than humans can currently detect.

There may also be things people imagine that do not exist.

The fact that we cannot currently tell which is true is a reason for investigation, not a reason to choose whichever answer we prefer.

**What we cannot observe should remain uncertain unless we have other good evidence about it.**
